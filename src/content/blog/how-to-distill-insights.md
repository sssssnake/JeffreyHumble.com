It's the end of a research project, and the presentation is tomorrow.

You've heard that your research insights should be inspiring, but your insights look like trash. Here is your first one:

> *1: "Users are complaining about the new UI update"*

🤨 While it might be true, it doesn't feel like an "Aha!" moment. It feels more like an "Mmhmm" moment.

What's missing:

- What are the users complaining about?
- Which users are complaining?
- What was it about the update that caused this?

*"Users are complaining about the new UI update"* is more of a finding than an insight. What's the difference?

### Findings vs. Insights

Oxford defines insight as *an accurate and deep understanding.* Wikipedia defines it as *the understanding of a specific cause and effect within a particular context.*

**One of the most** **common mistakes of novice researchers is thinking a finding is an insight**. Here's a handy breakdown from my [course on UX research](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/continuous-ux-research):

![findings vs insight](/images/f6cafb25-737b-4a65-b8e2-a36bef552c7e/Findings_vs_Insights-Jeff_Humble.png)

<p class="text-small center">[image by <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/jeff-humble/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Jeff Humble</a>]</p>

It's hard to tell when something is a finding vs. an insight. Hopefully, that chart helps. Let's revisit the example:

> *"Users are complaining about the new UI update."*

To take it to the insight level, we need to do more digging. Users seemed confused about our tool's UI, so let's look into the research to see if we can figure out "why." You know that Google's material design system heavily influenced the result of your UI update. Looking back at the interviews, you realize something startling: users actually think your tool is from Google! Hmmm, does the similarity to Google's UI cause users to have certain expectations about price? Many users expressed concern about the payment. After reviewing the data, you realize the similarity to Google in the users' eyes contains a real insight.

Let's put all of that new thinking together:

**Finding**: *"Users are complaining about the new UI update."*

**Finding**: *"Users think our tool is from Google."*

**Finding**: *"Users don't expect to pay for Google tools."*

⇣ which leads us to an epiphany…

**Insight**: *"Users think our new UI is a Google tool because it looks so similar, causing them to complain when the tool isn't free."*

Wow, now we’re getting to paradigm-shifting insight. Great insights make you slap your head and think, “Of course, that’s it! How could I possibly not see that!” Great insights seem obvious afterward.

What's different between our original finding and the new insight? We figured out "why" the finding was happening and looked deeper to find patterns of findings that might be transformational.

> The learning: you must "distill" your findings and research data to gain insight.

## How to Distill Research Insights

Brandy starts its life as wine, but after a special distillation process, you're left with a purified version of wine we call Brandy. Insights should go through a similar process.

![distill insights](/images/fb624170-b274-4a20-8137-c1065e9b6d2f/Distilling_research_insights-Jeff_Humble.png)

<p class="text-small center">[image by <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/jeff-humble/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Jeff Humble</a>]</p>

You might need many findings to get those precious few insights. This **process goes from** **quantity to quality.**

When trying to force a 3-month research project into 3-5 final insights overnight, you need to crank up the heat.

The magic in forming insights lies in what happens in the distillation activities.

<p class="text-large"><strong>Some ways to distill research insights:</strong></p>

<ul><li><p class="text-large">Cluster all of your findings together and re-organize them.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Look for the "why" behind your findings.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Cluster and re-sort your research data in a Miro board.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Talk through your findings with someone outside the project.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Do some desk research using the Pomodoro method.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Ask Chat GPT to re-write your findings until an idea sparks.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Make a quick visual to represent the finding/insight.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Do more research.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Review your research goals.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Do even more research.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Take a walk.</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Come back and keep editing.</p></li></ul>

Pick three from the list above and get started. It's important to start because no insights are worse than underwhelming insights. There's nothing worse than staring at a blank page when you need insights fast.

![](/images/dddc9e94-9c1b-4d8a-a042-a970eb714cc8/brandy-steve-mcqueen.gif)

Hopefully, you see how your findings can become delicious insights with a little time and effort.

## Insights Need Editing

If you haven’t noticed already, good insights are the result of iterative work. While the idea behind an insight might “pop into your head,” **great insights must be edited** **to reach their full potential.**

Just like any writing deliverable, it’s a process of iteration. Because just like any writing deliverable, your audience is the reader, not the writer. Insights are only as good as the audience they’re meant for. So if the reader of an insight doesn’t get it…it’s the fault of the insight, not the reader.

Some researchers think they’re King Midas 🤴👉💰…meaning they think that every insight they write turns to gold. Worse still, if stakeholders don’t “get” these pompous researchers’ insights, they would rather change stakeholders than change the insight.
