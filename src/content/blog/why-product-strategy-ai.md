Running a product company in 2025 means making decisions under constant uncertainty.

Markets are shifting, AI is changing the rules overnight, and competitors are burning through cash chasing the same customers.

In this environment, execution alone isn’t enough — what separates companies that survive from those that scale is a clear product strategy.

## Product Strategy in Times of Uncertainty

One of the most useful definitions of strategy is:

<blockquote><p class="text-large">“A general plan to achieve one or more long-term or overall goals under conditions of uncertainty.” -Wikipedia</p></blockquote>

Right now, uncertainty is everywhere. AI is disrupting workflows, reshaping products, and rewriting the competitive landscape. Founders and product leaders are under pressure to move fast — but without a clear product strategy, teams risk:

- Chasing competitors instead of differentiating
- Building features for everyone (and delighting no one)
- Burning resources on initiatives that don’t connect to long-term goals

With the right product strategy, you can focus your bets, align your team, and create a defensible position — even when the market feels unpredictable.

## What Product Strategy Is (and Isn’t)

![strategy diagram as a decision making framework](/images/12a16c95-af3f-442f-b863-3ed51fdc37ea/decision-making_framework.png)

Product strategy is **a decision-making framework that**:

<ul><li><p class="text-large">Guides product decisions across teams</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Aligns features and initiatives with long-term business goals</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Aligns with company strategy but gets specific with unique product team actions</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Creates offerings people actually choose and stick with</p></li></ul>

Product strategy is ***not***:

<ul><li><p class="text-large">A brainstorm full of nice-sounding ideas</p></li><li><p class="text-large">A feature list copied from competitors’ roadmaps</p></li><li><p class="text-large">A simple restatement of the business strategy</p></li><li><p class="text-large">Something you do every quarter</p></li></ul>

A strong product strategy should last for years, not quarters. Done right, it becomes the compass for product roadmaps, hiring, investment, and even positioning.

## Case Study: Nintendo’s Winning Move

To see product strategy in action, let’s rewind to the early 2000s.

Sony’s PlayStation 2 and Microsoft’s Xbox were dominating. Both targeted the same persona: 18–30-year-old men in dark dorm rooms, competing on graphics, hardware, and online play. They poured billions into the arms race for the same narrow audience.

![playstation xbox product strategy](/images/7bd7f03c-5705-4edb-ae25-9f67377577d4/isolated_gamers.png)

Nintendo, meanwhile, was struggling. Its GameCube underperformed, and the company needed a new direction.

**That’s where the product strategy came in.** They decided to do something different with their next console.

Instead of fighting the same fight, Nintendo zagged. Their product strategy was bold and different:

- Focus on family-friendly experiences instead of technical performance
- Prioritize simplicity and intuitiveness over complexity
- Encourage social, living-room play instead of solo gaming

The result was the Wii.

![wii product strategy market business](/images/5e80f0a7-f0d8-4303-8fa5-3c3c92276cce/demographic_of_wii.png)

With its motion-sensitive Wii Remote, easy-to-use interface, and inclusive games like Wii Sports and Wii Fit, Nintendo unlocked an entirely new market: families, casual players, even grandparents. A 97-year-old woman famously played her Wii every day.

![wii sales product strategy](/images/443ece26-9c9f-43a2-be1d-454ac8bbaa12/sales_of_wii.png)

The payoff? The Wii outsold both the Xbox 360 and PlayStation 3. By refusing to compete on specs, Nintendo created a new category and expanded the market far beyond “gamers.”

Lesson for founders and product leaders: great product strategy isn’t about building more features. It’s about building different.

## The Traps Product Leaders Fall Into

When startups and scale-ups set product direction, they often fall into familiar traps:

1. Confusing goals with strategy – “We want growth” isn’t a strategy. It’s an outcome.
2. Copying competitors – “They just launched X, so we need it too.” That’s not strategy, it’s reactive.
3. Chasing everything at once – Growth, AI, retention, new markets, brand. A laundry list isn’t a strategy.

![richard rumelt quote strategy obstacles](/images/b3b0517f-b133-4272-bd04-516f5f041b18/Richard_Rumelt_quote.png)

As strategist Richard Rumelt reminds us:

<blockquote><p class="text-large">“If you fail to identify and analyze the obstacles, you don’t have a strategy. You have a list of goals or wishes.”</p></blockquote>

## Practicing Better Product Strategy

Product strategy isn’t a deck you create once and file away; it’s a decision-making framework you return to constantly.

Founders and heads of product can sharpen their approach by:

- **Looking at the three Cs**: company strengths, customer needs, and competitor moves. Strategy comes from the intersection.
- **Framing challenges, not just goals:** The best strategies address systemic obstacles, not just targets.
- **Making trade-offs:** A strategy that tries to be everything ends up being nothing. Focus is power.
- **Connecting strategy to decisions**: Product strategy should guide choices on features, markets, hiring, and pricing.
- **Thinking in years, not sprints**: A strong product strategy should guide the roadmap for 2–5 years while adapting at the edges.

## Why Now Is the Moment

AI has created a wave of uncertainty — but also a wave of opportunity. Some companies will scramble to bolt AI features onto their products, blending into the noise. Others will step back, ask “What space can we own?”, and define a strategy competitors can’t or won’t match.

That’s the leverage of product strategy: it turns chaos into clarity, and volatility into advantage.
