So what happens to you, the person who used to do the making?

Do you hand it all over to the machine? Get a cabin in the woods? Or find a new way to work that complements what AI is good at...and bad at?

But the real question should be: When creating products and features gets five times faster, **what happens to that time you saved?**

## What Jevons Paradox tells us about the future of AI work

![jevons paradox ai](/images/a6e816dc-308b-41ba-a9db-0fcb32c0f812/Jevons_Paradox.png)

from [Strategy as the Human Layer](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/free-masterclass-strategy-human-layer-ai) by Jeff Humble

In the 1800s, economist William Jevons observed something counterintuitive: as steam engines became more efficient and used less coal per unit of work, Britain actually burned *more* coal overall. Why? Because efficiency made it cheaper. And thus, more people used it more often.

He called this counterintuitive idea the **Jevons Paradox**: ***efficiency improvements lead to increased consumption.***

Now apply this to knowledge work:

- Email made communication instant → we get more communications
- Photoshop made editing easier → clients request more revisions
- AI makes execution 10x faster → there will be more execution

In [**the talk**](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/free-masterclass-strategy-human-layer-ai), I asked my audience what they thought would happen when we get five times faster. The top two answers:

1. **You get 5x the feature requests** (same team, same timeframe, more work)
2. **Same features, but with 1/4 the team size**

Almost nobody picked "nothing changes, and you get more me-time at work."

Because here's the reality: **the organization will capture that time from you** unless you do something to protect your strategic time.

## You’re running faster, but in which direction?

You're probably going to be pressured to run faster in the coming years. But will you be running in the right direction?

This is where strategy becomes critical. And I don't mean strategy as some abstract executive thing. I mean strategy as **the human layer** that sits between your business goals and your AI tools.

Think of it like this:

- **Top layer**: Goals (business outcomes, UX goals, your career objectives)
- **Middle layer**: The human layer (where decisions get made)
- **Bottom layer**: AI tools and execution

If you don't strengthen that middle layer, the human layer, then you can easily be steered astray by what AI spits out. Your work could end up generic and rudderless.

## What human skills can complement AI?

![](/images/d04af1f9-1984-48c8-bc5c-216c09aae107/Human_Layer.png)

from [Strategy as the Human Layer](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/free-masterclass-strategy-human-layer-ai) by Jeff Humble

What does this human-friendly work in the human layer look like?

Here are 3 types of work that AI is bad at (and humans are good at) that you should master in the age of AI:

- **Framing**: What should we make?
- **Influencing**: How do we shape what gets made?
- **Aligning**: How do we keep our work coherent?

These three activities can be your human layer.

And if you put these together, magic happens: **Framing + Influencing + Aligning ≈ Strategy.**

It turns out these three things are a pretty good approximation of strategy.

I think that strategic thinking can be a good balance to the tactical speed that AI gives us for making. So, how do you strengthen these strategic skills while also learning new tactical AI skills?

You need to start thinking of your non-AI work as strategic. As you learn new AI skills, balance your career by also becoming a strategist.
